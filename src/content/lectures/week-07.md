Nothing about "one button" was ever a limit on how many things a press can
mean — only on how many things can be pressed at once. Context is where that
distinction stops being a slogan and starts being a mechanic: a single piece
of state decides which of the vocabulary built in weeks 1–6 currently
applies to the same press.

This week's studio exercise deliberately keeps the switch small — two
mechanics, one piece of state — because the hard part of context-switching
isn't picking mechanics, it's making the switch itself legible: a player
who's never seen either mode has to read which one is active from what's on
screen, not from trial and error. Two mechanics is enough to prove you can
do that cleanly. The Context Switch assessment later in the course asks for
the same technique again, unprompted, at three mechanics or more — by then
legibility gets harder precisely because there's more for a player to keep
straight, and that assessment is testing whether the technique still holds
once the switch stops being the easy case.

## Outline

- mode-dependent meaning: reinterpreting existing vocabulary through state,
  rather than adding to it
- why the studio exercise stops at two mechanics, and what specifically gets
  harder as that number grows
- legibility, not variety, as this week's actual design problem
